Painting Different Siding Types: Cedar Fiber Cement Vinyl

Lincoln County Painters

Every Siding Material Has Its Own Rules

Not all siding is created equal, and painting each type requires a different approach to preparation, product selection, and application. Using the wrong paint or technique on your specific siding material can lead to adhesion failure, premature peeling, or damage to the siding itself.

Across Lincoln County, you will find homes clad in cedar, fiber cement, vinyl, T1-11 plywood, and occasionally aluminum or hardboard. Here is what each type needs for a successful paint job.

Cedar Siding

Cedar is the most common siding material on older Oregon Coast homes. It is naturally beautiful and rot-resistant, but it requires careful handling when painting.

Preparation

Cedar produces tannins — natural oils that bleed through paint as dark brown stains. Any bare cedar must be primed with a stain-blocking primer before painting. Light sanding helps paint adhesion but avoid heavy sanding that opens the grain and increases tannin bleed.

Paint Selection

High-quality 100-percent acrylic latex paint works well on cedar. It flexes with the wood as it expands and contracts with moisture changes — something that happens constantly in coastal Oregon. Avoid elastomeric coatings on cedar, as they can trap moisture inside the wood.

For more on maintaining cedar, see our guide to cedar siding maintenance on the coast.

Fiber Cement Siding

Fiber cement (commonly known by the brand name HardiePlank) is increasingly popular in new construction and remodels along the coast. It is durable, fire-resistant, and handles moisture well — but it still needs proper painting.

Preparation

New fiber cement siding should be primed before installation. If it was installed unprimed, or if the factory primer has weathered, the siding needs a quality exterior primer. Fiber cement is porous and absorbs paint unevenly without proper priming.

Paint Selection

Acrylic latex paint is the recommended choice. Fiber cement manufacturers typically specify 100-percent acrylic latex for their warranty to remain valid. Two coats provide the best durability and appearance.

Vinyl Siding

Painting vinyl siding is possible, but it comes with specific limitations that homeowners should understand before committing.

Preparation

Vinyl must be thoroughly cleaned — usually with a pressure washer and cleaning solution — to remove all dirt, mildew, and chalky residue. No sanding is needed, but the surface must be completely clean for paint to adhere.

Color Limitations

The most important rule with vinyl siding: never paint it a color darker than the original. Vinyl expands and contracts with heat. Darker colors absorb more heat, causing the siding to warp, buckle, or distort. Choose a color that is the same shade or lighter than the existing color.

Paint Selection

Use paint specifically formulated for vinyl siding. These products are designed to flex with the material and resist peeling. Standard exterior paint may not adhere properly to vinyl's smooth surface.

T1-11 Plywood Siding

T1-11 is a grooved plywood siding found on many budget-friendly homes and outbuildings. It is more vulnerable to moisture damage than other siding types, making proper painting essential — especially in wet coastal climates.

Edges and grooves need particular attention, as these are where moisture enters and rot begins. Seal all edges with paint or primer, and ensure the bottom edge of each panel is not sitting in standing water or soil contact.

Matching the Approach to Your Home

Professional painters assess your specific siding type, condition, and exposure before recommending products and techniques. A home in Waldport with original cedar siding needs a different approach than a newer fiber cement home in Lincoln City.
