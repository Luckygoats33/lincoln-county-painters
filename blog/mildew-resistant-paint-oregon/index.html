Choosing Mildew-Resistant Paint for Oregon Homes

Lincoln County Painters

Why Mildew Is a Constant Challenge on the Oregon Coast

If you live anywhere in Lincoln County, you know mildew. The combination of persistent moisture, moderate temperatures, and limited direct sunlight on many surfaces creates ideal growing conditions. Mildew appears as dark spots or streaks on exterior surfaces and can show up on interior walls in rooms with poor ventilation.

The right paint can significantly reduce mildew growth, but not all paints offer the same level of protection. Understanding your options helps you make a better investment when it is time to repaint your home in Newport, Lincoln City, or anywhere on the coast.

Understanding Mildew and Paint

Mildew is a type of fungus that grows on surfaces where moisture is present. It feeds on organic material in and on paint films, including the oils and resins that make up the coating. On the Oregon Coast, mildew does not need much encouragement. Our climate provides the moisture, and the paint provides the food source.

Mildew is not just ugly. Over time, it can break down paint films and contribute to the deterioration of the substrate beneath. On wood siding, mildew growth often accompanies the early stages of wood decay.

What Makes Paint Mildew-Resistant

Built-In Mildewcides

Quality exterior paints include antimicrobial additives called mildewcides. These chemicals inhibit the growth of mildew and algae on the paint surface. Not all mildewcides are created equal. Some are more effective and longer-lasting than others. Premium paints typically use better mildewcide packages that remain active for more years.

Acrylic vs. Oil-Based

Acrylic latex paints generally resist mildew better than oil-based paints. Oil-based formulations contain organic oils that can serve as a food source for mildew. Acrylic formulations are less appetizing to fungi and tend to perform better in damp environments like ours.

Paint Sheen

Higher sheen paints resist mildew better than flat finishes. A satin or semi-gloss surface is smoother and sheds moisture more readily, giving mildew less to grip onto. For exterior trim and areas prone to mildew, consider using a satin or semi-gloss finish. For siding, a satin or low-luster finish offers a good balance between mildew resistance and appearance.

Surface Preparation for Mildew Prevention

Before painting over surfaces with existing mildew, the mildew must be killed and removed. Painting over live mildew is a common mistake that leads to rapid failure of the new paint. The mildew will grow right through the fresh coating.

A solution of bleach and water, or a commercial mildew cleaner, kills surface mildew. After cleaning, the surface needs to dry thoroughly before priming and painting. Your painter should use a primer with mildew-inhibiting properties to provide an additional layer of protection.

Areas That Need Extra Attention

Some parts of your home are more susceptible to mildew than others:

  • North-facing walls — These receive the least direct sunlight and stay damp longer
  • Under eaves and soffits — Sheltered from rain but not from humidity
  • Near landscaping — Plants close to the house trap moisture and block airflow
  • Lower portions of walls — Splash-back from rain and ground moisture contribute to damp conditions
  • Bathrooms and kitchens — Interior rooms with high humidity

Beyond Paint: Reducing Mildew Conditions

Paint is one part of the solution. You can also reduce mildew growth by:

  • Trimming vegetation back from exterior walls to improve airflow
  • Ensuring gutters and downspouts direct water away from the foundation
  • Running exhaust fans in bathrooms and kitchens to reduce interior humidity
  • Keeping crawl spaces ventilated and dry

On the Oregon Coast, you will never eliminate mildew entirely. But the right paint, proper preparation, and good maintenance practices can keep it under control.
