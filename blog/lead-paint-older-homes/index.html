Lead Paint in Older Homes: What Oregon Homeowners Should Know

Lincoln County Painters

Is There Lead Paint in Your Home?

If your home was built before 1978, there is a reasonable chance it contains lead-based paint somewhere — on exterior siding, interior trim, window frames, doors, or other surfaces. The federal government banned lead in residential paint in 1978, but homes painted before that date may have multiple layers of lead paint beneath the current finish.

Older homes in Newport, Toledo, and other established Lincoln County communities are most likely to contain lead paint. Homes built in the 1950s and 1960s are particularly likely, as lead content in paint was highest during that era.

Why Lead Paint Matters

Lead paint that is intact and in good condition — not peeling, chipping, or creating dust — is generally not an immediate hazard. The danger arises when lead paint is disturbed through sanding, scraping, demolition, or natural deterioration.

Lead dust and chips are toxic. Children are most vulnerable, but adults can also be affected by lead exposure. The health effects of lead poisoning include developmental delays in children, neurological damage, kidney problems, and other serious conditions.

The EPA RRP Rule

The Environmental Protection Agency's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule requires that any contractor working on homes built before 1978 must be EPA-certified and follow specific lead-safe work practices. This is federal law, not a guideline or recommendation.

What the Rule Requires

  • Firm certification: The painting company must be certified by the EPA as a lead-safe firm
  • Trained workers: At least one certified renovator must be assigned to each job
  • Lead-safe work practices: Specific containment, cleaning, and disposal procedures must be followed
  • Recordkeeping: Documentation of lead-safe practices must be maintained
  • Homeowner notification: Before work begins, the contractor must provide you with the EPA pamphlet about lead paint hazards

Testing for Lead Paint

Before painting or renovating a pre-1978 home, testing determines whether lead is present and where. Two common testing methods are available:

EPA-Recognized Test Kits

Swab-style test kits are available at hardware stores and provide immediate results. While they can give you a preliminary indication, they have limitations in accuracy. The EPA recognizes specific test kit brands for use under the RRP Rule.

Laboratory Testing

Paint chip samples can be sent to a certified laboratory for definitive analysis. This is the most accurate method and provides quantified results showing exactly how much lead is present.

Lead-Safe Painting Practices

When lead paint is present, painting over it is often safer than removing it — provided the existing paint is in stable condition. A coat of quality paint effectively encapsulates the lead paint beneath. However, any areas where lead paint is peeling, flaking, or deteriorated must be addressed using lead-safe methods:

  • Containing the work area with plastic sheeting
  • Minimizing dust creation by misting surfaces before scraping
  • Using HEPA-filtered vacuums for cleanup
  • Prohibiting dry sanding and open-flame burning of lead paint
  • Proper disposal of lead-contaminated debris

Choosing a Contractor for Older Homes

When hiring a painter for any home built before 1978, verify that the company holds current EPA RRP certification. Ask to see their certification and inquire about their lead-safe work practices. Any reputable contractor will be transparent about these credentials.
