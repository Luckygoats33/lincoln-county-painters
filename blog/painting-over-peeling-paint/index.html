How to Fix and Paint Over Peeling Paint

Lincoln County Painters

Why Paint Peels

Paint does not peel randomly. There is always an underlying cause, and identifying that cause is the first step toward a lasting repair. The most common reasons paint peels on Oregon Coast homes include:

  • Moisture behind the paint: The number one cause. Water getting behind the paint film — through leaks, condensation, or vapor drive — breaks the bond between paint and surface.
  • Poor surface preparation: Paint applied over dirty, chalky, or glossy surfaces without proper cleaning, sanding, or priming fails to adhere.
  • Incompatible paint layers: Latex paint applied over uncured oil-based paint, or vice versa without proper preparation, can cause delamination.
  • Age and weathering: All paint eventually breaks down from UV exposure and the elements. Coastal exposure accelerates this process.
  • Painting in poor conditions: Paint applied when surfaces are too hot, too cold, or damp does not form a proper bond.

Fixing the Underlying Problem

Before repainting, you must address whatever caused the peeling in the first place. Otherwise, the new paint will peel too — often within a year.

If moisture is the cause, trace the source. Common culprits include failed caulk around windows and doors, missing or damaged flashing, leaking gutters, poor grading that directs water toward the foundation, and interior humidity venting through exterior walls. Fix the moisture source before spending money on paint.

Proper Repair Process

Step 1: Remove Loose Paint

Scrape away all paint that is loose, peeling, bubbling, or flaking. Use a paint scraper and work until you reach paint that is firmly bonded. Do not stop at removing only the obviously loose areas — test surrounding paint by pressing the edge of a putty knife under it. If it lifts, scrape it too.

Step 2: Sand the Edges

Where scraped areas meet still-bonded paint, sand the edges smooth. This feathering eliminates the visible line between old paint and bare surface, creating a gradual transition that disappears under new paint.

Step 3: Clean the Surface

After scraping and sanding, clean the surface thoroughly. Remove all dust, debris, and any mildew or contamination. On the Oregon Coast, mildew is almost always present on exterior surfaces and must be killed with a mildew-cleaning solution.

Step 4: Prime Bare Areas

All bare wood or substrate exposed by scraping needs primer. This is non-negotiable. Primer seals the porous surface, provides adhesion for the topcoat, and blocks tannin bleed on cedar and other woods.

Step 5: Apply Topcoat

Once primed areas are dry, apply two coats of quality exterior paint across the entire surface — not just the repaired areas. Spot-painting creates visible patches that differ in color and sheen from the surrounding older paint. A full repaint ensures a uniform appearance.

When Peeling Is Too Extensive to Repair

If more than about a third of the paint on a wall or the entire house is failing, spot repairs become impractical. At that point, full removal of the failing paint — down to bare wood or a stable substrate — is necessary before repainting. This is significantly more labor-intensive and costly, but it produces a result that will last.

On homes in Depoe Bay, Waldport, and other oceanfront areas where salt exposure is heavy, extensive peeling may indicate that the previous paint system was not suitable for the exposure level. In these cases, upgrading to a more durable coating system as part of the repaint is advisable.
