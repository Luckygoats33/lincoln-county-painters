Cabinet Painting

The Professional Cabinet Painting Process Explained

Lincoln County Painters

Cabinet Painting Is Not Like Painting Walls

Cabinets are the hardest-working surfaces in your kitchen. They get opened and closed dozens of times daily, exposed to cooking grease and moisture, wiped down regularly, and subjected to impacts from everyday kitchen activity. The paint on cabinets needs to be far more durable than wall paint, and achieving that durability requires a more involved process.

Here is what professional cabinet painting looks like from start to finish.

Step 1: Assessment and Planning

Before any work begins, the cabinets are inspected for damage, loose hinges, warped doors, delaminating surfaces, and other issues that need to be addressed. The material type is identified — solid wood, MDF, plywood, or thermofoil — because each requires a slightly different approach.

Hardware is noted, numbering systems are planned for doors and drawers, and the scope of work is confirmed.

Step 2: Removal and Labeling

All doors, drawer fronts, and hardware are carefully removed. Each piece is numbered and labeled so it returns to its exact original position. Hinges are bagged and labeled. This prevents any confusion during reinstallation and ensures consistent alignment.

Step 3: Cleaning

Kitchen cabinets accumulate grease, cooking residue, and oils that paint will not stick to. Every surface — doors, drawer fronts, and cabinet boxes — is thoroughly cleaned with a degreasing solution. This step is critical. Painting over grease is the most common reason DIY cabinet paint jobs fail.

Step 4: Sanding and Surface Preparation

All surfaces are sanded to create a profile for the primer to grip. On previously finished or varnished cabinets, this also removes the glossy surface that would prevent adhesion. Sanding also smooths any imperfections, grain raise, or minor damage.

After sanding, surfaces are vacuumed and wiped clean to remove all dust particles. Even tiny dust specks will show through a smooth cabinet finish.

Step 5: Filling and Repair

Dents, dings, nail holes, and grain on oak or other open-grained woods are filled with appropriate filler. If the goal is a perfectly smooth, grain-free finish on oak cabinets, a grain filler is applied and sanded smooth. This step can add significant time but produces a dramatically smoother result.

Step 6: Priming

A high-adhesion primer is applied to all surfaces. For cabinets, the primer must bond tenaciously and provide a foundation for the topcoat. Shellac-based or bonding primers are common choices because they seal the surface, block tannin bleed, and provide excellent adhesion.

After priming, surfaces are lightly sanded again with fine-grit sandpaper to ensure a smooth base for the topcoat.

Step 7: Topcoat Application

The topcoat is where the magic happens. Professional cabinet painters typically apply two coats of a durable finish — either a high-quality acrylic alkyd enamel or a waterborne polyurethane enamel. These products dry harder than standard wall paint and resist scuffing, staining, and moisture.

Application is usually done with a spray system for the smoothest possible finish. HVLP (High Volume, Low Pressure) sprayers produce a factory-like finish without brush marks or roller texture.

Between coats, surfaces are lightly sanded to ensure intercoat adhesion and to knock down any minor imperfections.

Step 8: Reinstallation

Once the final coat has fully cured — which can take several days — doors and drawer fronts are carefully reinstalled. Hardware is reattached or replaced with new hardware if the homeowner has chosen to upgrade. Hinges are adjusted for proper alignment, and doors are checked for smooth operation.

The Professional Difference

This process takes one to two weeks and requires specialized equipment, products, and technique. The result is cabinets that look and feel like new — with a smooth, durable finish that stands up to daily kitchen use.
