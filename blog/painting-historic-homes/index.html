Painting Historic Homes: Preservation and Care

Lincoln County Painters

Preserving History Through Proper Painting

Lincoln County has homes dating back to the late 1800s and early 1900s, particularly in Newport, Toledo, and Lincoln City. These historic properties are part of the community's character and heritage. Painting them requires special knowledge, techniques, and respect for their architectural significance.

Lead Paint: The First Consideration

Any home built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint. The EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule requires that contractors working on pre-1978 homes be EPA Lead-Safe certified and follow specific work practices to prevent lead contamination.

This is not optional or flexible. Lead paint creates a serious health hazard, particularly for children, when it is disturbed by sanding, scraping, or demolition. A qualified contractor will:

  • Test painted surfaces for lead before disturbing them
  • Set up proper containment if lead is present
  • Use lead-safe work practices during preparation
  • Clean up according to EPA standards
  • Properly dispose of lead-contaminated waste

Before hiring a painter for a pre-1978 home, verify their EPA Lead-Safe certification. This certification is separate from the Oregon CCB license.

Preserving Architectural Details

Historic homes often feature decorative elements that give them their character: ornate trim, shingle patterns, bracket details, turned porch columns, window casings with distinctive profiles, and other period-specific features. These details are what make historic homes special, and preserving them is a key part of any painting project.

Repair Before Replacing

Whenever possible, repair original woodwork rather than replacing it. Epoxy wood consolidants can restore rotted wood to structural integrity. Dutchman repairs replace only the damaged portion of a board. Custom millwork can replicate missing or damaged details. Replacing original materials with modern alternatives changes the character of the home.

Hand Work Over Spray

Historic homes with detailed trim and multiple surfaces are typically best served by brush and roller application rather than spray. Brush application allows the painter to work paint into complex profiles, avoid overspray on adjacent surfaces, and maintain control over coating thickness on intricate details.

Period-Appropriate Colors

Historic homes look best in colors appropriate to their era and style. While you are not required to use period colors on a private residence (unless local historic district regulations apply), choosing colors that complement the home's architecture creates the most harmonious result.

Several paint manufacturers offer curated historical color palettes organized by architectural era. These can provide a starting point for your color selection. Local historical societies may also have information about original colors used on homes in your area.

Special Substrate Considerations

Old-Growth Wood

Many historic homes were built with old-growth lumber that is denser and more durable than modern wood. This material can last indefinitely if properly maintained, but it has different painting characteristics than new-growth wood. Old-growth cedar and fir absorb primers and paints differently and may need specific product selections.

Plaster Walls

Interior walls in older homes are often plaster over lath rather than drywall. Plaster can develop hairline cracks that need to be stabilized before painting. Use a flexible primer that bridges small cracks, and consider specialty products designed for historic plaster surfaces.

Multiple Paint Layers

A century-old home may have dozens of paint layers built up on its surfaces. This buildup can cause adhesion problems as the combined weight of the paint film becomes too heavy. In some cases, selective removal of old paint is necessary for a new coat to adhere properly.

Working on the Oregon Coast

Historic homes on the coast face the same environmental challenges as newer construction, with the added complexity of older materials and construction techniques. Exterior painting on a historic coastal home requires balancing preservation of the home's character with the need for weather-resistant coatings.

The good news is that many of the traditional painting materials and techniques used on these homes originally were well-suited to the coastal environment. Quality oil-based primers and acrylic latex topcoats provide excellent protection while maintaining compatibility with historic surfaces.

Finding the Right Contractor

Not every painting contractor has experience with historic homes. Look for a contractor who demonstrates knowledge of lead-safe work practices, respect for original architectural details, and experience with the particular challenges of older construction. Your historic home deserves a contractor who sees it as a treasure to be preserved, not just another surface to cover.
