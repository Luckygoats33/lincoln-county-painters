Wind-Driven Rain: A Coastal Reality

The Oregon Coast is one of the windiest and wettest stretches of coastline in the lower 48 states. From fall through spring, storms push rain horizontally against your home's exterior with force that ordinary drip patterns cannot compare to. Wind-driven rain finds every gap, crack, and weak point in your home's protective envelope.

For homeowners in Newport, Depoe Bay, Lincoln City, and throughout Lincoln County, understanding how wind-driven rain damages paint helps you protect your home more effectively.

How Wind-Driven Rain Causes Paint Failure

Normal rainfall runs down the surface of your home vertically. Gravity pulls it away, and properly functioning gutters channel it off the building. Wind-driven rain behaves differently. It hits surfaces horizontally and can even push upward under eaves and soffits. This rain:

  • Forces water into joints and seams that vertical rain would not reach
  • Pushes moisture behind siding laps and into end grain of wood boards
  • Saturates surfaces more deeply because impact pressure drives water into the paint film
  • Reaches areas that are usually protected, like under window sills and behind trim boards

Once moisture gets behind the paint film or into the substrate, it causes peeling, blistering, wood rot, and mildew. These problems often show up on the side of the home facing the prevailing storm direction, which on the Oregon Coast is typically the south and west.

Preparation Strategies That Work

Caulking and Sealing

The single most important step in protecting against wind-driven rain is proper caulking. Every joint, seam, and gap where water could enter needs to be sealed with a high-quality, flexible, paintable caulk. This includes around windows, where trim meets siding, at corner boards, and anywhere different materials meet. On the coast, use caulk rated for high-moisture environments and expect to inspect and maintain it annually.

Flashing and Weather Barriers

Paint alone cannot stop wind-driven rain. Proper flashing above windows and doors, behind trim boards, and at transitions between materials is essential. If flashing is missing or damaged, water will get behind your siding regardless of how good the paint is. A thorough exterior painting project should include an inspection of flashing and weather barriers.

Priming Exposed Wood

Any bare wood exposed during preparation should be primed immediately. End grain and cut edges are especially vulnerable because they absorb water much faster than face grain. A quality exterior wood primer seals these surfaces and provides a foundation for the topcoat.

Paint Selection for Wind-Driven Rain

Choose an exterior paint with strong adhesion and flexibility. A paint that bonds tightly to the surface and moves with the wood as it expands and contracts is less likely to crack and let water in. One hundred percent acrylic latex paints with high film builds perform well in this regard.

Two coats of topcoat are the minimum for coastal homes. Some painters add a third coat on the most exposed wall, which provides additional thickness and protection where it is needed most.

Maintenance After Painting

A good paint job is your best defense, but it is not maintenance-free. Walk around your home at least once a year and look for:

  • Caulk that has cracked, shrunk, or pulled away
  • Paint that is beginning to chip or peel, especially at joints
  • Gaps that have opened up between boards
  • Standing water or staining below windows and sills

Catching small issues early and touching them up prevents larger failures. On the coast, an annual inspection after the winter storm season is good practice.
