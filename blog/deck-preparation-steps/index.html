How to Prepare Your Deck for Staining

Lincoln County Painters

Why Deck Preparation Matters More Than the Stain

If you have ever seen a deck where the stain peeled, flaked, or wore unevenly within a year, the cause was almost certainly poor preparation. The stain itself is only as good as the surface beneath it. On the Oregon Coast, where decks face rain, fog, salt air, and UV exposure, thorough preparation is the difference between a stain that lasts three years and one that fails in six months.

Step 1: Inspect the Deck

Walk your entire deck and look for problems that need addressing before any stain goes on.

  • Loose or damaged boards — Replace any boards that are split, warped beyond repair, or structurally compromised. Secure loose boards with deck screws.
  • Popped nails or screws — Reset any fasteners that have risen above the surface. Countersink screws slightly below the wood surface.
  • Rot — Probe suspect areas with a screwdriver. If the wood feels soft or crumbles, that section needs replacement.
  • Mildew or algae growth — Dark spots, green film, or fuzzy growth all need treatment during cleaning.
  • Existing stain condition — Is the old stain peeling, flaking, or simply fading? Peeling stain needs more aggressive removal than faded stain.

Step 2: Clear the Deck

Remove all furniture, planters, grills, and accessories. Move items well away from the deck so they do not get splashed during cleaning. Cover nearby plants and landscaping with plastic sheeting if you are using chemical cleaners.

Step 3: Clean the Surface

Cleaning removes dirt, mildew, oxidized wood fibers, and any remnants of old finish. There are two main approaches:

Wood Cleaner and Brightener

Apply a deck cleaning solution with a pump sprayer, let it work for the recommended time, then scrub with a stiff brush and rinse. Follow with a wood brightener to restore the pH balance and open the wood grain. This two-step process produces the best results for stain absorption.

Pressure Washing

A pressure washer speeds up cleaning but requires care. Use a fan tip nozzle and keep the pressure under 1,500 PSI for softwoods like cedar and redwood. Maintain a consistent distance and overlap your strokes to avoid leaving marks. Never hold the nozzle in one spot, as this gouges the wood.

Many professionals use a combination: apply cleaner, let it dwell, then use moderate pressure to rinse.

Step 4: Sand the Deck

After the deck has dried from cleaning, sanding creates a smooth, even surface for stain absorption.

  • Use 60 to 80 grit sandpaper for heavily weathered or rough boards
  • Use 80 to 100 grit for general smoothing
  • A random orbital sander works well for deck boards
  • Hand sand railings, balusters, and hard-to-reach areas
  • Always sand in the direction of the wood grain

Sanding also removes any wood fibers that were raised during pressure washing, giving you a surface that stain can penetrate evenly.

Step 5: Let the Deck Dry Completely

This step trips up a lot of homeowners. After cleaning and sanding, the wood needs to dry thoroughly. In the dry summer months along the coast, this usually takes 48 to 72 hours. If fog or morning dew is heavy in your area, such as Depoe Bay or Waldport, you may need to wait longer.

Test by sprinkling water on the surface. If it beads up, the wood is not ready. If it soaks in within a few seconds, you are good to proceed.

Step 6: Protect Surrounding Areas

Before applying stain, tape off any surfaces you do not want stained: house siding, door thresholds, metal flashing. Lay drop cloths on any surfaces below the deck. Cover nearby concrete or stone that could be permanently stained by drips.

The Payoff of Proper Prep

All of this work happens before a single drop of stain touches the wood. It is the unglamorous part of deck staining, but it is what separates a professional result from a weekend disappointment.
