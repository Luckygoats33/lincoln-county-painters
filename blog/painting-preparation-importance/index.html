Why Preparation Is 80% of a Good Paint Job

Lincoln County Painters

The Work You Cannot See Makes All the Difference

Ask any experienced painter what separates a paint job that lasts from one that fails, and the answer is always the same: preparation. The actual act of applying paint to a wall is the visible, satisfying part of the job. But the hours of cleaning, sanding, patching, priming, and masking that happen before the first coat are what determine whether that paint stays put for years or starts failing within months.

What Preparation Includes

Surface Cleaning

Paint cannot adhere properly to dirty surfaces. Dust, grease, mildew, salt deposits, and chalky residue from old paint all prevent the new coating from bonding. On the Oregon Coast, this step is especially critical because of the salt and mildew that accumulate on exterior surfaces.

For interiors, walls may look clean but often have a film of cooking grease, dust, or hand oils that need to be washed away. Kitchen walls and bathroom surfaces almost always need a degreasing wash before painting.

Removing Loose Paint

New paint applied over loose, peeling, or flaking old paint will fail quickly. The new coating is only as strong as what it is attached to. All loose paint must be scraped, sanded, or otherwise removed down to a sound surface. This can be the most time-consuming part of exterior preparation, especially on older coastal homes where moisture has worked under the existing paint.

Repairing Damage

Cracks in plaster or drywall, holes from picture hangers, nail pops, damaged wood, rotted siding, and deteriorated caulking all need to be repaired before painting. Painting over damage does not fix it; it just hides it temporarily while the underlying problem continues to worsen.

On coastal homes, check for wood rot around windows, door frames, and anywhere water may have penetrated. Repairing these areas before painting prevents further deterioration and ensures your new coating has a solid substrate.

Sanding

Sanding serves multiple purposes. It smooths patched areas so they blend with surrounding surfaces. It creates a slight texture on glossy surfaces that helps new paint adhere. On exterior surfaces, it feathers the edges where old paint meets bare wood, creating a smoother transition that is less visible under the new topcoat.

Priming

Primer is the bridge between the surface and the topcoat. It seals porous surfaces, blocks stains, provides uniform adhesion, and creates a consistent base color for the topcoat. Different surfaces require different primers: new drywall, bare wood, metal, stained surfaces, and glossy surfaces each need a primer formulated for that specific situation.

Skipping primer is one of the most common shortcuts, and it always shows in the final result. Bare spots flash through, stains bleed through, and adhesion suffers.

Masking and Protection

Protecting surfaces that should not be painted, such as trim, hardware, windows, floors, and fixtures, is a preparation step that directly affects the finished appearance. Clean, crisp paint lines come from proper masking. Overspray and drips on unprotected surfaces look sloppy and can be difficult or impossible to fully clean up.

Why Shortcuts Fail

Every preparation step exists to solve a specific problem. When a step is skipped, the problem it would have prevented shows up sooner or later:

  • Skip cleaning, and paint peels because it cannot bond to the contaminated surface
  • Skip scraping, and new paint lifts along with the loose paint underneath
  • Skip repairs, and damage continues behind the new paint, eventually causing bigger failures
  • Skip priming, and you get uneven coverage, stain bleed-through, and poor adhesion
  • Skip masking, and you spend hours cleaning up mistakes or live with sloppy edges

How to Evaluate a Painter's Preparation

When comparing painting estimates, pay attention to how much detail each contractor provides about preparation. A detailed estimate that lists specific preparation steps signals a contractor who understands their importance. A vague estimate that jumps straight to "paint two coats" may indicate a contractor who plans to cut corners on prep.

Ask your contractor what percentage of the project time they expect to spend on preparation versus actual painting. For most residential painting projects, preparation represents at least half the total labor time, and often more.

The Bottom Line

Preparation is not glamorous, and it is not the part of a paint job that gets photographed. But it is the work that determines whether your paint job looks great and lasts for years or starts failing within months. Every dollar spent on thorough preparation saves multiple dollars on premature repainting.
