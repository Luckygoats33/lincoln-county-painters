Exterior Painting

Exterior Painting Built for the Oregon Coast

Salt air, driving rain, and UV exposure demand more than a standard paint job. We prepare, prime, and finish exteriors to withstand what the coast throws at them.

Surface Preparation

The Foundation of Every Exterior Paint Job

An exterior paint job is only as good as the surface underneath it. On the Oregon Coast, that surface has likely been exposed to months of rain, salt spray, moss, mildew, and UV damage. Skipping or rushing preparation means the new paint will fail prematurely. We do not skip it.

Pressure washing is the first step. We use controlled pressure appropriate to the siding type — lower for older cedar, higher for concrete and fiber cement. The goal is to remove dirt, mildew, chalking old paint, and salt deposits without damaging the substrate. We allow adequate drying time before moving to the next step, which on the coast may mean waiting a full day or more depending on conditions.

Scraping and sanding follow. Loose, peeling, or alligatored paint is scraped back to a sound edge. We feather sand transitions between bare wood and intact paint to create smooth surfaces. On homes with multiple layers of old paint, we sometimes find original coats from decades ago that have finally let go — all of that must come off.

Caulking seals gaps around windows, doors, trim joints, and siding seams. On coastal homes, these joints work harder because wood expands and contracts more with the constant moisture cycling. We use paintable, flexible exterior caulk rated for the temperature and humidity range.

Priming bare wood and repaired areas before the finish coats. We prime every surface that has been scraped to bare substrate. On cedar and redwood, we use stain-blocking primer to prevent tannin bleed. On previously painted surfaces in good condition, spot priming may be sufficient.

Coastal Challenges

What the Coast Does to Exterior Paint

Painting a home on the Oregon Coast is fundamentally different from painting a home in the Willamette Valley, Portland, or Central Oregon. Here is why.

Salt Air Corrosion

Airborne salt from the Pacific accelerates paint breakdown. It is hygroscopic — it attracts and holds moisture against surfaces, creating conditions for blistering, peeling, and substrate rot. Properties within a half-mile of the surf line experience the most aggressive exposure. Even homes farther inland in Lincoln County catch salt-laden wind, especially during winter storms with onshore flow.

UV Exposure

The coast gets less total sunshine than inland Oregon, but UV exposure is deceptive. Reflected light off the ocean amplifies UV on west- and south-facing walls. Fog diffuses light but does not block UV. Over time, UV breaks down the binder in paint, causing chalking, fading, and loss of adhesion. South- and west-facing walls consistently fail before north- and east-facing ones.

Persistent Moisture

Lincoln County receives roughly 70 inches of rain annually, and many days are overcast with high relative humidity even when it is not raining. Exterior surfaces stay damp longer than in drier climates, which slows drying and curing of new paint and promotes mildew growth on existing finishes. Proper product selection — paints formulated to cure in high humidity — is essential.

Wind-Driven Debris

Strong onshore winds carry sand, salt mist, and organic debris. During winter storms, sustained winds regularly exceed 40 mph on exposed headlands and beachfront properties. This wind creates micro-abrasion on paint surfaces over time and drives moisture into seams and joints that might stay dry in calmer climates. Paint films need to be flexible and well-adhered to resist it.

Product Selection

Paints and Coatings for Coastal Durability

We do not use the cheapest paint available, and we do not use the most expensive just because it costs more. We select products based on what the surface needs and what the exposure conditions demand.

100% acrylic latex is our standard for most exterior wood and fiber cement siding. Acrylic paint stays flexible as it ages, which matters because coastal temperature and moisture cycles cause wood to expand and contract constantly. Rigid oil-based paints tend to crack and peel faster in these conditions. Quality acrylic formulas also resist UV fading and chalking better than lower-grade products.

Elastomeric coatings are an option for stucco, masonry, and concrete surfaces, or for wood siding with chronic cracking. These thick, rubberized coatings bridge hairline cracks and form a waterproof membrane. They are more expensive per gallon and take more material to apply, but on the right surfaces they significantly extend the life of the finish.

Mildew-resistant additives are standard in every exterior product we use. Mildew is nearly inevitable on the Oregon Coast — the combination of moisture, moderate temperatures, and organic material creates ideal growing conditions. Using paint with built-in mildewcide does not prevent it forever, but it extends the time before mildew becomes visible.

We carry products from several major manufacturers and will recommend specific lines based on the project. We are not exclusive to one brand.

Siding Types

We Paint Every Type of Siding on the Coast

Lincoln County homes feature a wide range of exterior materials. Each one requires different preparation and product choices.

Cedar Lap Siding

The most common siding in older coastal homes. Cedar is naturally rot-resistant but requires proper priming to prevent tannin bleed. We scrape, sand, spot-prime bare wood, and apply two full coats of acrylic exterior paint.

Cedar Shingle

Common on cottages and Cape Cod-style homes throughout the county. Shingles can be painted or stained. We assess the condition of each face and replace any shingles that have split or rotted before refinishing.

Fiber Cement (Hardie)

Increasingly common in new construction and remodels. Fiber cement is dimensionally stable and rot-resistant, but factory primer can deteriorate if left exposed too long. We clean, spot-prime as needed, and apply quality acrylic paint.

T1-11 Plywood Siding

Found on many 1970s-1990s homes throughout Lincoln County. T1-11 is vulnerable to moisture at the edges and bottom courses. We repair swelling and delamination, seal exposed edges, prime, and paint or stain.

Wood Shake

A rustic exterior material seen on some older and custom-built homes. Similar treatment to cedar shingle — we assess condition, replace deteriorated pieces, and apply penetrating stain or solid-body paint depending on the owner's preference.

Composite & Vinyl Trim

We paint composite trim boards, PVC trim, and vinyl siding components. These materials require specific adhesion primers and paint formulations rated for the substrate. We do not paint vinyl siding with dark colors that may cause heat warping.

Timing

When Is the Best Time to Paint an Exterior on the Coast?

The ideal window for exterior painting in Lincoln County is roughly late June through mid-October, when rain is least frequent and temperatures stay above 50 degrees Fahrenheit consistently. That said, the Oregon Coast rarely gets long stretches of perfect weather even in summer. We monitor forecasts carefully and schedule work around rain. We need at least two to three consecutive dry days for proper application and curing. Spring and early fall can work for exterior painting, but the margin for weather interruptions is tighter.

Longevity

How Long Does Exterior Paint Last on the Coast?

This is the most common question we get, and the honest answer is: it depends on the surface, the exposure, the product, and the quality of preparation.

A well-prepared and properly painted exterior in an inland Oregon location might last 10 to 15 years before showing significant wear. On the coast, the same surface and product combination will typically show wear sooner — anywhere from 5 to 10 years, depending on how exposed the property is.

West-facing and south-facing walls take the most punishment and will need attention first. These surfaces catch the brunt of wind-driven rain, salt spray, and afternoon UV. It is not unusual for these sides of a house to need repainting while the north and east sides still look good.

Oceanfront properties on bluffs or within a few hundred yards of the surf line face the most aggressive conditions. Salt spray, constant wind, and unobstructed sun exposure all accelerate paint degradation. These properties may need exterior work every 5 to 7 years.

Sheltered inland properties — homes in Toledo, Siletz, or protected valleys — get more life from their exterior paint because they are shielded from direct salt air. These homes may approach the 10-year mark with good products and thorough preparation.

The single biggest factor in longevity is preparation. Paint applied over properly cleaned, scraped, sanded, and primed surfaces adheres better and lasts longer than paint applied over shortcuts.
