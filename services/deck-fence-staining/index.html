Outdoor Wood Care

Deck & Fence Staining

Thorough prep. Coastal-rated products. Outdoor wood protection that stands up to the Oregon Coast.

Before We Open a Can

Every Project Starts with a Wood Assessment

Outdoor wood on the Oregon Coast takes a beating. Before any stain goes on, we need to understand what we are working with. Applying finish over compromised wood is a waste of time and money, so the first step is always a thorough hands-on inspection.

We check every board, post, and railing for the conditions that determine whether your deck or fence is ready for stain or needs repair first:

  • Structural integrity — soft spots, rot, splitting, and fastener corrosion
  • Previous coating condition — peeling, flaking, worn bare, or still adhered
  • Mold, mildew, and algae growth — common on coastal wood that stays damp
  • Graying and UV damage to the wood surface
  • Ground contact and drainage — areas that stay wet longer deteriorate faster

If we find boards that need replacing, we will let you know before any staining begins. There is no point staining a board that will fail in six months.

Surface Preparation

Strip, Clean, Sand, Brighten

Preparation is the difference between a stain job that lasts and one that peels. We spend more time prepping than we spend actually applying stain, and that is by design. On coastal wood especially, cutting corners on prep leads to early failure.

Our prep process follows a methodical sequence:

  • Stripping — If there is old finish that is cracking or peeling, we chemically strip it. You cannot stain over a failing coating.
  • Pressure washing — We use controlled pressure to remove dirt, mildew, and loose wood fibers without damaging the surface. Too much pressure gouges softwood. We dial it in for the specific species.
  • Brightening — After washing, wood often looks dull or gray. An oxalic acid brightener opens the pores and restores the natural color so the stain absorbs evenly.
  • Sanding — On decks and railings especially, we sand rough spots and raised grain to create a smooth, even surface for the finish coat.
  • Drying — Wood needs to dry thoroughly before staining. We typically allow 48 to 72 hours after washing, depending on weather conditions.
Choosing the Right Finish

Penetrating Stain vs. Film-Forming Stain

The type of stain matters as much as the brand. On the coast, the choice between penetrating and film-forming products is a decision that directly affects how often you will need to re-stain.

FactorPenetrating StainFilm-Forming Stain
How it worksSoaks into wood fibers, protects from withinSits on the surface like a thin paint film
AppearanceNatural wood look, shows grainMore uniform color, higher opacity options
Moisture handlingAllows wood to breathe and dry — key for coastal moisture cyclesTraps moisture beneath the film, can blister in wet climates
Failure modeFades and wears gradually — no peelingPeels and cracks, requiring full strip before re-coat
MaintenanceClean and re-apply over existing coatStrip, sand, and re-apply when it fails
Best for coastRecommended for most horizontal surfaces (decks, deck rails, benches)Can work on vertical surfaces (fences, siding) with lower moisture exposure

For most deck surfaces on the Oregon Coast, we recommend a high-quality penetrating oil stain. It handles the constant moisture cycling better than film-forming products. For fences and vertical surfaces with less direct weather exposure, either type can work well depending on the look you want.

Coastal Reality

What the Oregon Coast Does to Outdoor Wood

Living on the coast means your deck and fence face conditions that inland properties simply do not. Understanding these challenges is why local experience matters.

Salt Air Corrosion

Airborne salt accelerates the breakdown of wood fibers and corrodes metal fasteners. Properties closer to the ocean — Nye Beach, Depoe Bay, the Roads End bluff — see this more aggressively. We select stains with UV blockers and mildewcides formulated for marine-adjacent environments.

Rain and Moisture

Lincoln County averages over 70 inches of rain per year. Wood absorbs and releases moisture constantly, expanding and contracting with each cycle. This movement is what causes stain failure. Penetrating products that move with the wood dramatically outlast rigid film coatings in this environment.

UV Degradation

Even on overcast days, UV radiation breaks down lignin in wood and degrades stain pigments. South-facing and west-facing decks take the hardest hit. We use stains with strong UV-blocking pigments and recommend semi-transparent or solid-color finishes for the most exposed surfaces.

Moss and Algae

The damp, shaded micro-climates common to coastal properties are ideal growing conditions for moss, lichen, and algae. These organisms trap moisture against the wood surface and accelerate decay. Our prep includes killing and removing all biological growth before staining.

Wind-Driven Weather

The Oregon Coast is windy. Wind-driven rain reaches surfaces that would stay dry in calmer climates — the undersides of railings, sheltered fence sections, the back sides of pergola posts. We coat exposed surfaces thoroughly, including areas that are easy to skip.

Timing the Weather Window

Staining on the coast requires watching the forecast carefully. We need a window of dry weather — ideally three to four consecutive days — for prep, application, and cure time. Late summer and early fall typically offer the most reliable conditions.

Materials We Work With

Wood Types and How We Approach Each One

Not all wood species respond to stain the same way. The species of your deck or fence determines our prep method, product selection, and expected results.

  • Western Red Cedar — Common on older coastal decks and fences. Naturally resistant to decay but softens and grays over time. Takes penetrating stain beautifully. Requires careful pressure washing — too aggressive and you gouge the soft grain.
  • Pressure-Treated Pine — The most common modern decking lumber. Needs to dry and weather for several weeks before accepting stain. We verify moisture content before application.
  • Redwood — Less common locally but found on some properties. Similar approach to cedar. Natural tannins can bleed through lighter stain colors, so we plan product selection accordingly.
  • Composite Decking — Not a candidate for traditional staining. If your composite deck has faded or stained, we can discuss specialized composite refinishing options, but we are upfront that it is a different process with different expectations.
Not Just Decks

Railings, Pergolas, and Other Outdoor Structures

Decks and fences get the most attention, but plenty of other outdoor wood needs protection too. We stain and finish all types of exterior wood structures:

  • Deck railings and balusters — Often the first thing to show wear because of constant hand contact and exposure from all sides
  • Pergolas and arbors — Horizontal beams collect standing water; we ensure proper product and application to handle this
  • Garden structures — Raised beds, trellises, planter boxes
  • Gazebos and covered structures — Even sheltered wood needs protection on the coast
  • Outdoor stairs and landings — High-traffic horizontal surfaces that need slip-resistant, durable finishes
  • Retaining walls — Timber retaining walls in ground contact need specialized products

If it is outdoor wood and it needs protection, we can help.

Maintenance Planning

How Often Should You Re-Stain on the Coast?

Staining is not a one-time job. On the Oregon Coast, outdoor wood needs regular maintenance to stay protected and looking good.

Coastal Properties (Within a Few Miles of the Ocean)

Decks and fences exposed to salt air, heavy rain, and direct weather typically need re-staining every 2 to 3 years. Properties with direct ocean exposure — like the Bayfront in Newport or bluff homes in Depoe Bay — may need attention on the shorter end of that range. The constant moisture cycling and salt accelerate finish wear.

Inland Properties (Toledo, Siletz, Protected Areas)

Wood structures a few miles from the ocean, sheltered from direct salt air, hold their finish longer. Expect 3 to 5 years between re-staining, depending on sun exposure and drainage. You will still see moss and algae growth, but the salt corrosion factor is significantly reduced.

We can set up a maintenance reminder so you do not have to guess. When the stain starts to look thin — run a water test by splashing a little water on the surface. If the water soaks in instead of beading, it is time to re-coat.
